Before I managed a team, before I wrote production code, I was a competitive programmer. I took part in more than 100 online and onsite contests and solved over 1,500 problems on Codeforces, LeetCode and HackerRank. Today I lead a team of 8 engineers at Eagle 3D. These two worlds look very different, but more carried over than I expected.

## What carried over

### Breaking big problems into small ones

A hard contest problem is rarely solved in one leap. You find a smaller version you *can* solve, then build up. Planning a project works the same way: a vague client goal becomes milestones, then tasks small enough for one person to own and finish.

### Edge cases are where things break

In contests, the solution that passes the examples often fails on hidden tests: an empty input, a huge number, a duplicate value. In production it's the same. Most of the bugs I've helped debug were edge cases nobody thought about: an unusual network, an unexpected client setup, a rare sequence of actions. I still ask "what's the strangest input this will see?" in every review.

### Calm under time pressure

A contest clock teaches you to stay calm, prioritise and avoid panic changes. That helps a lot when a production issue lands and a client is waiting. Our team aims to respond to issues within 12 hours, and a steady, methodical first response matters more than a fast, rushed one.

### Debugging is a method, not luck

Wrong answer on test 37? You don't guess; you narrow it down. Reproduce, isolate, test a hypothesis, repeat. The same discipline works for a broken integration across a client's system and ours.

## What did *not* carry over

### Working alone

Contests are mostly solo. Real software is built by teams, and as a manager my output is the team's output. The biggest shift for me was moving from "how do I solve this?" to "how do I help someone else solve this?"

### Clever code

In a contest, short, clever code wins. In production, clear code wins. Someone else has to read it, change it and support it for years. I had to unlearn some contest habits as an engineer.

### Perfect information

Contest problems are precisely defined. Client requirements almost never are. A lot of my work now is asking the right questions *before* anyone writes code, and getting answers written down.

### Communication is the work

No contest scores you on how well you explain your solution. In a team, a great fix that nobody understands is a risk. Writing clear updates, documentation and feedback has become one of the most important parts of my job, whether with my team or with our 10+ international clients.

## Would I recommend competitive programming?

Yes, as long as you treat it as training rather than the goal. It builds problem-solving speed, rigour and resilience. Pair it with real projects, teamwork and communication, and it becomes a strong foundation for engineering and for leading engineers.
