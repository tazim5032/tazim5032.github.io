I spent almost two years as a software engineer at Eagle 3D before moving into management. That shapes how I lead: I remember what made my own work easier, and what got in the way. Today I lead a team of 8 engineers working with 10+ international clients. This post describes how I run day-to-day engineering work and keep a remote team healthy.

> My job as a manager is to make it easy for good engineers to do good work, and to make sure clients always know where things stand.

## 1. Make priorities impossible to misread

Most delivery problems I've seen start with unclear priorities, not weak engineering. So the first thing I do is keep a single, visible list of what matters now, what comes next, and what is deliberately *not* being worked on.

- Every task has one owner and a clear definition of done.
- When a client request arrives mid-sprint, I decide where it fits before it reaches an engineer, so people aren't pulled in two directions.
- If priorities change, I explain *why*. People handle change well when they understand the reason.

## 2. Write things down, especially across time zones

Our team works remotely with clients in different countries. Writing is what keeps that working. As an engineer I wrote documentation for APIs, SDKs and platform features, and I saw how much a clear written explanation saves compared with another meeting.

- Progress updates are written, short and structured: done, in progress, blocked.
- Decisions get recorded where the team can find them later, with the reasoning.
- Meetings are for discussion and decisions, not status updates that could have been a message.

## 3. Treat blockers as the most urgent work

An engineer waiting on an answer is the most expensive thing on a team. We aim to respond to every issue within 12 hours, and I check in on blockers first, every day, and treat removing them as my main job. That can mean getting a decision from a client, clarifying a requirement, or pairing on a stubborn integration issue.

Because I came from debugging production and integration problems myself, I can often help narrow down a technical issue quickly, or at least know who to bring in.

## 4. Protect quality without slowing everyone down

Shipping fast doesn't help if it means more bugs in production later. I keep quality steady with a few simple habits:

- **Review progress against requirements early**, not just at the end, so misunderstandings surface while they're cheap to fix.
- **Reproduce before fixing.** Competitive programming and my time as an AI Trainer at Scale AI taught me to confirm a problem and check edge cases before trusting a fix.
- **Give feedback that is specific and actionable.** Rating AI responses against detailed guidelines taught me the difference between "this is wrong" and "this fails because of X; here is what good looks like."

## 5. Be the bridge between clients and engineers

Clients want confidence and predictability. Engineers want clear requirements and focus. A big part of my role is translating between the two.

- I keep clients updated before they need to ask, including when something is at risk.
- I turn client feedback into concrete, prioritized tasks rather than passing on raw messages.
- I shield the team from churn, without hiding context they need to make good decisions.

## 6. Keep the team healthy

A team that is constantly firefighting will eventually burn out, however talented it is. I try to keep the pace sustainable:

- Regular one-on-one conversations, so problems come up early and people feel heard.
- Credit given publicly and specifically when someone does great work.
- When something goes wrong, we look at *what* failed and how to prevent it, not *who* to blame.
- Room to learn, because the tools we use, from web frameworks to real-time 3D streaming, keep changing.

## What I'm still working on

Moving from writing code to leading people is a real shift, and I'm still learning. The hardest part is letting go of solving every problem myself, and instead helping someone else solve it. I'd rather grow a team that can handle things without me than be the bottleneck.
